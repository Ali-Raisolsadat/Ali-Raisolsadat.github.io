% SLIDE 2
\begin{frame}{Why This Chapter Matters}
Statistical inference uses information from a \textbf{sample} to learn about a larger \textbf{population}.

\vspace{0.2cm}
However, different random samples from the same population generally produce different values of a statistic. This natural sample to sample variation is called \textbf{sampling variability}.

\begin{block}{Central Idea}
Chapter 13 examines how statistics vary across repeated random samples. Understanding this variation allows us to judge how reliably a sample statistic estimates a population parameter.
\end{block}

By the end of the chapter, you should be able to answer the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item What is the difference between a parameter and a statistic?
  \item What is a sampling distribution?
  \item Why do larger samples generally produce more stable statistics?
  \item When can a Normal model be used for a sample mean or a sample proportion?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 3
\begin{frame}{Parameters and Statistics}
A \textbf{parameter} is a numerical characteristic of a population.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is fixed for a particular population
  \item It is often unknown because measuring the entire population may be impractical
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.15cm}
A \textbf{statistic} is a numerical characteristic calculated from sample data.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It can be calculated once the sample has been observed
  \item Its value may change when a different random sample is selected
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.15cm}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{
  >{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}
  >{\centering\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}
  >{\centering\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}}
\toprule
Measure & Sample statistic & Population parameter \\
\midrule
Mean & $\bar{x}$ & $\mu$ \\
Proportion & $\hat{p}$ & $p$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A statistic is used to estimate or learn about the corresponding population parameter.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 4
\begin{frame}{Three Different Distributions}
Three related but distinct distributions appear throughout this chapter.

\begin{block}{Population Distribution}
The distribution of a variable for all individuals in the population
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Sample Distribution}
The distribution of the observed values for individuals in one particular sample
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Sampling Distribution}
The distribution of a statistic calculated from all possible random samples of the same size from the population
\end{block}

\textbf{Note}: Population and sample distributions describe \textbf{individual observations}. A sampling distribution describes the possible values of a \textbf{statistic}.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 5
\begin{frame}{Connecting the Three Distributions}
\begin{center}
\lectureimage[0.9\linewidth]{fig_ch13_three_distributions.png}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.1cm}
We begin with a population, select a random sample and calculate a statistic. Repeating this process many times produces the sampling distribution of that statistic.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 6
\begin{frame}{Example 13.1: Identifying Population Parameters}
\begin{block}{Question}
A national registration system contains complete records for every birth in the population of interest during one year. Among all registered births, $8\%$ were classified as low birth weight. Among all people giving birth for the first time during that year, the mean age was $26.3$ years. Are these values parameters or statistics? Give the appropriate notation.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Solution}
Both values describe entire populations defined by the study rather than samples. Therefore, both are \textbf{parameters}. 

The population proportion of low-birth-weight births is $p=0.08$

The population mean age at first birth is $\mu=26.3$
\end{block}

Whether a value is a parameter or a statistic depends on where the data came from, not on the size of the numerical value.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 7
\begin{frame}{Example 13.2: Identifying Sample Statistics}
\begin{block}{Question}
A polling organization selects a random sample of $1023$ adults. In the sample, $19\%$ report smoking cigarettes during the previous week. Among the sampled adults who reported smoking, the mean number of cigarettes smoked per day is $12$. Are these values parameters or statistics? Give the appropriate notation.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Solution}
Both values were calculated from sample data. Therefore, both are \textbf{statistics}.

The sample proportion reporting cigarette use is $\hat{p}=0.19$

The sample mean number of cigarettes smoked per day among the sampled smokers is $\bar{x}=12$
\end{block}

These statistics describe the observed sample and may be used to estimate corresponding parameters in the target population.
\end{frame}

% SECTION 2
\section{The Sampling Distribution of \texorpdfstring{$\bar{x}$}{xbar}}

% SLIDE 8
\begin{frame}{Repeated Sampling and the Sampling Distribution}
A statistic is not fixed before a random sample is selected. Its value depends on which individuals happen to be included in the sample.

\vspace{0.2cm}
If we repeatedly select random samples of the same size, the value of the statistic will vary from sample to sample.

\begin{block}{Sampling Distribution}
The sampling distribution of a statistic is the probability distribution of that statistic over all possible random samples of the same size selected using the same sampling process.
\end{block}

\textbf{Note}: The observations in a sampling distribution are \textbf{statistics calculated from samples}, not measurements from individual members of the population.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 9
\begin{frame}{Examples 13.3 and 13.4: Simulating Sample Means}
\begin{block}{Setting}
Individual IQ scores are modelled by a Normal distribution with $\mu=100$ and  $\sigma=15$. A resaercher uses R to select $500$ random samples, each containing $n=25$ IQ scores.
\end{block}

For each simulated sample, the following process is used:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Select $25$ IQ scores from the population
  \item Calculate the sample mean $\bar{x}$
  \item Record the value of $\bar{x}$
  \item Repeat the process until $500$ sample means have been obtained
\end{enumerate}

The $500$ recorded sample means approximate the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ for samples of size $25$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Examples 13.3 and 13.4: Simulating Sample Means}
\begin{center}

\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Examples 13.3 and 13.4: Simulating Sample Means}
The simulated sampling distribution has three important features:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It is centred near the population mean of $100$
  \item It has an approximately Normal shape
  \item It has considerably less spread than the distribution of individual IQ scores
\end{itemize}

Individual IQ scores have a standard deviation of $15$, but the $500$ sample means have a standard deviation close to $4$. Sample means vary less than individual observations.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 11
\begin{frame}{The Sampling Distribution of $\bar{x}$}
Suppose a random sample of size $n$ is selected from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.

\small
\begin{block}{Centre}
The mean of the sampling distribution equals the population mean
$$\mu_{\bar{x}}=\mu$$
Therefore, $\bar{x}$ is an \textbf{unbiased estimator} of $\mu$. Unbiased does not mean that every sample mean equals $\mu$. It means that the sample means are centred at $\mu$ over repeated random samples.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Spread}
The standard deviation of the sampling distribution is
$$\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
This quantity describes the typical sample-to-sample variation in $\bar{x}$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 12
\begin{frame}{How Sample Size Affects the Sample Mean}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
% Left column: image
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\centering
\lectureimage[0.85\linewidth]{fig_ch13_sample_size.png}
\end{column}

% Right column: explanation
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
As the sample size increases:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The centre of the sampling distribution remains at $\mu$
	\item The spread of the sampling distribution decreases
	\item The sample mean is more likely to fall close to the population mean
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\begin{block}{Main Idea}
Larger random samples produce more stable and more precise estimates of the population mean.
\end{block}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 13
\begin{frame}{Why the Square-Root Relationship Matters}
The standard deviation of the sample mean is
$$\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
Therefore, the improvement in precision depends on the \textbf{square root} of the sample size. If the sample size is multiplied by $4$, then
$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{4n}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
The standard deviation of $\bar{x}$ is divided by $2$. If the sample size is multiplied by $100$, then
$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{100n}}=\frac{1}{10}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
The standard deviation of $\bar{x}$ is divided by $10$.

\textbf{Note}: To reduce the standard deviation of $\bar{x}$ by a factor of $c$, the sample size must be multiplied by $c^2$. Larger samples improve precision but the improvement becomes increasingly expensive.
\end{frame}

% SECTION 3
\section{Shape and the Central Limit Theorem}

% SLIDE 15
\begin{frame}{When Is the Sampling Distribution Normal?}
The shape of the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ depends on both the shape of the population distribution and the sample size.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The Population is Normal: If the population distribution is Normal, then the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is \textbf{exactly Normal} for every sample size $n$.
	\item The Population is not Normal: If the population distribution is not Normal, the Central Limit Theorem tells us that the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ becomes \textbf{approximately Normal} as the sample size increases.
\end{enumerate}

There is no single sample-size requirement that works for every population. Strong skewness and extreme outliers generally require a larger sample.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 18
\begin{frame}{The Central Limit Theorem}
\begin{block}{Central Limit Theorem}
Suppose independent observations are selected from a population with finite mean $\mu$ and finite standard deviation $\sigma$. As the sample size $n$ increases, the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ becomes approximately Normal, with
$$\mu_{\bar{x}}=\mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\end{block}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{alertblock}{What the Theorem Says}
The distribution of \textbf{sample means across repeated samples} becomes more nearly Normal as $n$ increases.
\end{alertblock}
\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{alertblock}{What It Does Not Say}
The theorem does not say that the population or the observations within one large sample become Normal.
\end{alertblock}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 19
\begin{frame}{Example 13.6: The Central Limit Theorem in Action}
\begin{block}{Question}
Suppose a population distribution is strongly right-skewed. How does the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ change as the sample size increases?
\end{block}

A sample mean combines information from several observations. As more observations are included in each mean, the effect of individual unusually large values becomes less dominant. As the sample size increases
\begin{itemize}
	\item The sampling distribution becomes less right-skewed
  	\item Its shape becomes more nearly Normal
  	\item Its centre remains at $\mu$
  	\item Its standard deviation decreases according to
  	$$\sigma_{\bar{x}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\end{itemize}
The population remains right-skewed. It is the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ that becomes approximately Normal.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 20
\begin{frame}{Assessing Whether a Normal Approximation Is Reasonable}
There is no universal minimum sample size that guarantees an appropriate Normal approximation for every population. The assessment should consider the following features together:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The available information about the shape of the population distribution
  	\item The size of the random sample
  	\item The presence of substantial skewness or unusual observations
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{General Guidance}
\begin{itemize}
	\item A population that is approximately symmetric and has no substantial outliers may require less averaging
  	\item A population with substantial skewness or outliers may require a larger sample before the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately Normal
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

Sample size should not be considered by itself. The shape of the population distribution also affects how quickly the Normal approximation becomes reasonable.
\end{frame}

\section{The Sampling Distribution of \texorpdfstring{$\hat{p}$}{phat}}

% SLIDE 22
\begin{frame}{The Sample Proportion}
For a categorical variable with two possible outcomes, the statistic of interest is often the \textbf{sample proportion}. Let
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n$ represent the sample size
  \item $X$ represent the number of observations in the category of interest
\end{itemize}

The sample proportion is
$$\hat{p}=\frac{X}{n}$$
\begin{block}{Population and Sample Notation}
\begin{itemize}
	\item $p$ represents the proportion in the population
  	\item $\hat{p}$ represents the proportion observed in the sample
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}


% SLIDE 23
\begin{frame}{The Sampling Distribution of $\hat{p}$}
Suppose a random sample of size $n$ is selected from a population in which the proportion belonging to the category of interest is $p$.

\textbf{Centre}: The mean of the sampling distribution is
$$\mu_{\hat{p}}=p$$

\textbf{Spread}: The standard deviation is
$$\sigma_{\hat{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}$$

\textbf{Conditions}: The observations should be obtained through an appropriate random process. When sampling without replacement, a common condition is
$$n\leq 0.10N$$

\textbf{Normal Approximation}: The sampling distribution of $\hat{p}$ is approximately Normal when
$$np\geq10 \qquad n(1-p)\geq10$$
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 24
\begin{frame}{Example 13.8: Contact Lens Practices}
\begin{block}{Question}
A random sample of $281$ contact-lens users contains $139$ people who report that they do not consistently wash their hands before handling their lenses. What is the sample proportion?
\end{block}

Define a success as a sampled person reporting that they do not consistently wash their hands before handling their contact lenses. The number of successes and the sample size are
$$X=139\qquad\text{and}\qquad n=281$$
Therefore
$$\hat{p}=\frac{X}{n}=\frac{139}{281}\approx0.495$$
Approximately $49.5\%$ of the people in the sample reported that they did not consistently wash their hands before handling their contact lenses.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 25
\begin{frame}{Examples 13.9 and 13.10: Blood Type O}
\begin{block}{Question}
Suppose the population proportion of people with blood type O is $p=0.45$. A random sample of $n=1000$ records is selected from a large national registry. What is the probability that the sample proportion with blood type O is $0.42$ or lower?
\end{block}

Step 1 -- Identify the Sampling Distribution: The centre is
$$\mu_{\hat{p}}=p=0.45$$
The standard deviation is
$$\sigma_{\hat{p}}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.45(0.55)}{1000}}\approx0.01573$$
The expected counts are
$$np=1000(0.45)=450$$
$$n(1-p)=1000(0.55)=550$$
Both are greater than $10$ so a Normal approximation is appropriate.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 26
\begin{frame}{Examples 13.9 and 13.10: Blood Type O}
Step 2 -- Express the Probability: The probability of interest is
$$P(\hat{p}\leq0.42)$$

Step 3 -- Standardize: The corresponding standardized value is
$$z=\frac{\hat{p}-p}{\sigma_{\hat{p}}}=\frac{0.42-0.45}{0.01573}\approx-1.907$$

Step 4 -- Calculate and Interpret: Using the standard Normal distribution
$$P(\hat{p}\leq0.42)=P(Z\leq-1.907)\approx0.0283$$

There is approximately a $2.83\%$ probability that a random sample of $1000$ records contains a sample proportion of $0.42$ or lower when the population proportion is $0.45$.
\end{frame}

\section{The Law of Large Numbers}

% SLIDE 29
\begin{frame}{The Law of Large Numbers}
\begin{block}{Law of Large Numbers}
For independent observations from a population with a finite mean $\mu$, the sample mean approaches the population mean as the sample size increases
$$\bar{x}_n \longrightarrow \mu$$

For independent Bernoulli observations with a common probability $p$, the sample proportion approaches the population proportion
$$\hat{p}_n \longrightarrow p$$
\end{block}

\textbf{Note}: The Law of Large Numbers describes an overall long-run tendency. It does not state that each additional observation must move the statistic closer to the parameter.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 30
\begin{frame}{The Law of Large Numbers in Action}
\centering
\lectureimage[0.98\linewidth]{fig_ch13_lln.png}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 31
\begin{frame}{Why the Law of Large Numbers Matters}
The Law of Large Numbers provides a mathematical explanation for why averages and proportions tend to become more stable as the number of observations increases.

\vspace{0.15cm}
This long run stability is relevant in settings such as:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Summarizing repeated physical or biological measurements and/or simulations
  	\item Estimating average claim costs across a large insurance portfolio
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Connection to Sampling Distributions}
The Law of Large Numbers describes what happens as the sample size becomes very large. A sampling distribution describes how much a statistic varies for a particular finite sample size.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 13.11: Repeated Laboratory Measurements}
A laboratory repeatedly measures the protein concentration of a reference sample. The reference concentration is $50$ micrograms per millilitre. Suppose each measurement can be represented as
$$M_i=50+\varepsilon_i$$
where the measurement errors $\varepsilon_i$ are independent, have mean $0$ and have standard deviation $0.8$ micrograms per millilitre. What happens to the running mean
$$\bar{M}_n=\frac{M_1+M_2+\cdots+M_n}{n}$$
as the number of measurements increases?

\pause

\begin{block}{Solution}
Because the measurement process is centred at $50$
$$\bar{M}_n\longrightarrow50$$
as $n$ increases.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 13.11: Repeated Laboratory Measurements}
For independent measurements with mean $50$ and standard deviation $0.8$
$$E(\bar{M}_n)=50$$
and
$$\sigma_{\bar{M}_n}=\frac{0.8}{\sqrt{n}}$$

For $n=25$ measurements
$$\sigma_{\bar{M}_{25}}=\frac{0.8}{\sqrt{25}}=0.16$$

For $n=500$ measurements
$$\sigma_{\bar{M}_{500}}=\frac{0.8}{\sqrt{500}}\approx0.0358$$

Averaging more measurements reduces the effect of random measurement error. The exact sequence will differ from one simulation to another, but the running mean tends to stabilize near $50$.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 37
\begin{frame}{Example 13.12: Claim Costs in an Insurance Portfolio}
Let $C_i$ represent the annual claim cost for policy $i$. Under the illustrative model,
$$E(C_i)=\$1020$$

If the policy outcomes are independent and follow the same probability model, the Law of Large Numbers gives
$$\bar{C}_n\longrightarrow\$1020$$
as the number of policies increases.

\begin{block}{Risk Pooling}
The cost of an individual policy remains uncertain. However, the average cost per policy becomes more stable when costs are averaged across a large portfolio.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 38
\begin{frame}{Example 13.12: Claim Costs in an Insurance Portfolio}
\begin{alertblock}{Important Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
	\item The Law of Large Numbers does not guarantee that the average will equal $\$1020$ in a particular year
	\item Policy outcomes may not be independent when many claims result from the same storm, wildfire or other shared event
	\item A large portfolio does not correct an inaccurate probability model or systematic changes in claim patterns
\end{itemize}
\end{alertblock}

The expected claim cost is only one component of an insurance premium. Administrative expenses, uncertainty and other financial considerations may also be included.
\end{frame}
