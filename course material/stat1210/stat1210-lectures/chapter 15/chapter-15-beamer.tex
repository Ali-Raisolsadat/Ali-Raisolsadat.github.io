\section{From Probability to Inference}

\begin{frame}{Probability and Inference}
Chapters 9--12 developed probability models: rules for events, random variables and the Normal, binomial and Poisson distributions.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Chapters 13--14 used those models to describe how a statistic varies from sample to sample and introduced confidence intervals and tests.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Chapter 15 connects the two halves: \textbf{every confidence interval and every $P$-value is a probability calculated from a distribution.}

\begin{block}{Central Connection}
Probability describes what data could occur under a model. Inference compares the observed data with those model-based possibilities.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parameters, Statistics and Randomness}
A population parameter is a fixed but usually unknown number. A sample statistic is calculated from the observations and changes from sample to sample.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Statistic} \\
\midrule
Population mean & $\mu$ & $\bar{x}$ \\
Population probability & $p$ & $\hat p$ \\
Population event rate & $\lambda$ & Observed rate or count \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{Before} sampling, the statistic is a random variable with a distribution. \textbf{After} sampling, it is one known number.

\vspace{0.1cm}
Inference uses that one number while accounting for how much it \emph{could} have varied.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Population Distribution vs.\ Sampling Distribution}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{population distribution} describes individual values
  \item The \textbf{sample distribution} describes the values in one sample
  \item The \textbf{sampling distribution} describes a statistic across all possible samples of the same size
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
If individual values have standard deviation $\sigma$, the mean of $n$ independent values has
$$\sigma_{\bar{X}}=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Probability Rules Inside Inference}
Several rules from Chapters 9--10 appear directly in inferential calculations.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Complement rule:} $P(Z\ge z)=1-P(Z<z)$, and $P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})$
  \item \textbf{Addition rule:} a two-sided $P$-value adds two disjoint tails
  \item \textbf{Conditional probability:} a $P$-value is calculated \emph{given} that $H_0$ and the model are true
  \item \textbf{Independence:} knowing one observation does not change the probabilities for another; this justifies $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ and binomial/Poisson models
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Important Distinction}
$P(\text{data as or more extreme}\mid H_0)$ is not the same as $P(H_0\mid\text{data})$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Normal Distribution in Inference}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
Chapter 11 standardized \emph{observations}. Inference standardizes a \emph{statistic}:
$$z=\frac{\text{statistic}-\text{model mean}}{\text{SD of the statistic}}$$
For $\bar{x}$, the SD is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Central areas} give critical values for intervals
  \item \textbf{Tail areas} give $P$-values for tests
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_ch15_critical_values.png}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Matching the Probability Model to the Sample}
The sample determines the starting probability model.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} >{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.27\textwidth} X}
\toprule
\textbf{Response} & \textbf{Possible model} & \textbf{Parameter} \\
\midrule
Continuous measurement & Normal model, or Normal sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ & Population mean $\mu$ \\
Successes in $n$ trials & Binomial model & Success probability $p$ \\
Event count over exposure & Poisson model & Event rate $\lambda$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
A model is more than a shape. A binomial model assumes fixed $n$, independent trials and a common $p$. A Poisson model assumes independent events at a stable rate with no clustering.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exact Models and Normal Approximations}
An inferential probability can come from the original model or from a Normal approximation.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.25\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Approach} & \textbf{Advantage} & \textbf{Important condition} \\
\midrule
Exact binomial or Poisson calculation & Retains the discrete probability model & The model assumptions must fit the data-generating process \\
Normal approximation & Uses standardization and Normal tail areas & Large enough sample, e.g.\ $np\ge10$ and $n(1-p)\ge10$ for a binomial count \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
``Exact'' refers to the calculation under the model. It does not guarantee that the model itself is correct. We compare the two approaches on real data in Example 15.3.
\end{frame}

\section{Conditions for Inference}

\begin{frame}{Conditions for One-Sample $z$ Procedures}
Every calculation is only as trustworthy as the model behind it. The one-sample $z$ procedures for a population mean assume that
\begin{itemize}
  \item The observations come from a random sample or a defensible random process
  \item The observations are independent
  \item The population is Normal, or the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately Normal
  \item The population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known
\end{itemize}
$$\bar{x}\pm z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \qquad\text{and}\qquad z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$$
Knowing $\sigma$ is rare in practice; Chapter 17 replaces it with $s$. The questions in this chapter still apply.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the Data Come From}
Probability enters a study through randomization. The two kinds support different conclusions.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.22\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Design feature} & \textbf{How chance is used} & \textbf{Conclusion supported} \\
\midrule
Random sampling & Individuals selected from a population by chance & Generalize to the sampled population \\
Random assignment & Units assigned to treatments by chance & Causal treatment effect \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Random sample, no assignment: generalization but not causation
  \item Randomized experiment on volunteers: causation for participants, limited generalization
  \item Convenience sample, observational: neither
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Independence of Observations}
Independence means that knowing one observation does not change the probabilities for another.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Dependence arises when
\begin{itemize}
  \item Several measurements come from the same individual
  \item Animals share a litter or enclosure, or patients share a clinic
  \item Measurements come from nearby locations or adjacent times
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Sampling without replacement creates slight dependence, which is negligible when
$$n\le0.10N \qquad \text{(the 10\% condition)}$$
The 10\% condition does \emph{not} fix clustering or repeated measurements; those need methods that model the structure.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practical Problems After Data Collection Begins}
Even a well-planned study can encounter difficulties.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nonresponse} can make respondents systematically different from nonrespondents
  \item \textbf{Attrition} creates bias when participants who leave differ from those who remain
  \item \textbf{Measurement error} introduces systematic error outside the sampling model
  \item \textbf{Complex designs} (clustering, strata, unequal selection probabilities) invalidate a simple random-sample formula
\end{itemize}
A probability calculation cannot correct a fundamental mismatch between the study design and the statistical model.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sample Size and the Normal Approximation}
The $z$ procedures need the \emph{sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$} to be approximately Normal. How large $n$ must be depends on the population shape.
\begin{itemize}
  \item With a small sample, strong skewness or outliers can make Normal-based inference unreliable
  \item A moderate sample is often enough when the population is roughly symmetric with light tails
  \item With a large sample, the Central Limit Theorem gives an approximately Normal $\bar{x}$ under broad conditions
\end{itemize}
There is no universal cutoff such as $n=30$. Stronger skewness and heavier tails require more observations. Plot the data first.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Outliers and Nonresistant Statistics}
$\bar{x}$ and $s$ are not resistant. One outlier can shift the estimate, inflate the standard deviation and change the conclusion of a test. 

\vspace{0.2cm}
Before performing inference:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Check for data-entry and measurement errors
  \item Examine a suitable graph of the observations
  \item Investigate scientifically plausible reasons for unusual values
  \item Consider another method when a small sample contains severe skewness or influential outliers
\end{itemize}
An observation should not be removed solely because its removal changes the conclusion.
\end{frame}

\section{How Confidence Intervals Behave}

\begin{frame}{The General Form of a Confidence Interval}
Many confidence intervals have the form
$$\text{estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}$$
The margin of error combines a critical value with the standard deviation of the estimate:
$$\text{margin of error}=\text{critical value}\times\text{SD of the estimate}$$
For a one-sample $z$ confidence interval for a population mean,
$$m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
The estimate determines the centre. The margin of error determines the half-width.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Quantities Control the Margin of Error}
$$m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Change} & \textbf{Effect on $m$} \\
\midrule
Critical value $z^*$ & Higher confidence & Wider interval \\
Population variability $\sigma$ & Larger $\sigma$ & Wider interval \\
Sample size $n$ & Larger $n$ & Narrower interval \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
The next slides take these one at a time, using the same IQ example throughout.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lever 1: Confidence Level and the Critical Value}
The critical value $z^*$ leaves the chosen confidence level in the centre of the Standard Normal distribution.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Confidence} & \textbf{Central area} & \textbf{Area in each tail} & \textbf{$z^*$} \\
\midrule
$90\%$ & $0.90$ & $0.05$ & $1.645$ \\
$95\%$ & $0.95$ & $0.025$ & $1.960$ \\
$99\%$ & $0.99$ & $0.005$ & $2.576$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
This is the same area reasoning used for Normal probabilities in Chapter 11. Higher confidence moves the cutoffs farther into the tails, so $z^*$ grows.

\vspace{0.1cm}
Lower confidence gives a narrower interval, but it is wrong more often. It adds no information.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.2: Confidence Level and Margin of Error}
A random sample of $n=31$ of adult males has mean IQ $\bar{x}=105.84$. Assume $\sigma=15$, so
$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}=\frac{15}{\sqrt{31}}\approx2.694$$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Confidence} & \textbf{$z^*$} & \textbf{Margin $z^*(2.694)$} & \textbf{Interval} \\
\midrule
$90\%$ & $1.645$ & $4.43$ & $(101.41,\ 110.27)$ \\
$95\%$ & $1.960$ & $5.28$ & $(100.56,\ 111.12)$ \\
$99\%$ & $2.576$ & $6.94$ & $(98.90,\ 112.78)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
All three intervals share the same centre because they use the same sample mean.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.2: Comparing Confidence Levels}
\slidefig[0.66]{fig_ch15_confidence_levels.png}
Higher confidence leaves more probability in the central Normal area, so the interval must extend farther from the estimate.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lever 2: Population Variability}
The margin of error is directly proportional to $\sigma$:
$$m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\begin{itemize}
  \item Doubling $\sigma$ doubles the margin of error
  \item A smaller $\sigma$ produces less sample-to-sample variation in $\bar{x}$
  \item A larger $\sigma$ makes the population mean harder to estimate precisely
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lever 3: Sample Size and the Square-Root Relationship}
For a fixed confidence level and population standard deviation,
$$m\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Change in sample size} & \textbf{New $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$} & \textbf{Effect on margin} \\
\midrule
$n\rightarrow2n$ & $1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.707$ as large & Margin shrinks by about $29\%$ \\
$n\rightarrow4n$ & One-half as large & Margin is halved \\
$n\rightarrow9n$ & One-third as large & Margin is divided by $3$ \\
$n\rightarrow100n$ & One-tenth as large & Margin is divided by $10$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
To halve the margin of error, you need \emph{four times} as many observations.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.2: Increasing the Sample Size}
At $95\%$ confidence with $\sigma=15$:
\begin{align*}
m_{31}&=1.960\frac{15}{\sqrt{31}}\approx5.28 \\
m_{62}&=1.960\frac{15}{\sqrt{62}}\approx3.73 \qquad \frac{m_{62}}{m_{31}}=\sqrt{\frac{31}{62}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx0.707 \\
m_{124}&=1.960\frac{15}{\sqrt{124}}\approx2.64 = \frac{5.28}{2}
\end{align*}
Doubling $n$ cuts the margin by about $29.3\%$, not $50\%$. Quadrupling $n$ halves it.

\vspace{0.15cm}
A new sample would usually also change $\bar{x}$, and therefore the interval centre.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Margin of Error and Sample Size}
\slidefig[0.66]{fig_ch15_margin_sample_size.png}
The largest gains in precision occur when the sample is small. Later increases still help, but each additional observation buys less.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed 95\% Confidence, Different Sample Sizes}
\slidefig[0.64]{fig_ch15_intervals_sample_size.png}
\small
Each interval is $105.84\pm1.96(15)/\sqrt{n}$. The confidence level, and so the long-run capture rate, stays at $95\%$ for every $n$. Only the \textbf{precision} changes: larger $n$ gives a narrower interval with the same coverage.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Margin of Error Does and Does Not Measure}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\begin{block}{Covered}
Random sample-to-sample variation generated by the stated sampling process
\end{block}
The margin comes from the sampling distribution, which describes only that one source of uncertainty.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\begin{alertblock}{Not covered}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Undercoverage and voluntary response
  \item Nonresponse and attrition
  \item Leading questions, inaccurate self-reports
  \item Systematic measurement error
  \item Confounding
\end{itemize}
\end{alertblock}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{0.2cm}
A narrow interval means high precision under the model. It does not guarantee the estimate is free of bias.
\end{frame}

\section{How Hypothesis Tests Behave}

\begin{frame}{A $P$-Value Is a Probability from the Null Distribution}
A confidence interval describes plausible parameter values. A test assumes \emph{one} value, $H_0$, and asks how unusual the data would be.

\vspace{0.15cm}
The \textbf{null distribution} is the sampling distribution of the test statistic when $H_0$ and the model are true.
$$P\text{-value}=P(\text{statistic at least as extreme as observed}\mid H_0)$$
\begin{itemize}
  \item Small: the observed result lies in an unusual part of the null distribution
  \item Large: the observed result is reasonably compatible with the null model
\end{itemize}
The calculation evaluates the data under $H_0$. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Test Statistic Locates the Data}
For a one-sample $z$ test of a population mean
$$z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$$
\begin{itemize}
  \item The numerator is the observed difference from the null value
  \item The denominator is the SD of $\bar{x}$ expected under repeated sampling
  \item $z$ measures the difference in those units: $z=2.4$ means $\bar{x}$ is $2.4$ SDs above $\mu_0$
\end{itemize}
The $P$-value is the tail area beyond the observed $z$ in the null distribution $N(0,1)$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Alternative Hypothesis Determines the Tail}
The direction of the scientific question determines which outcomes count as ``at least as extreme.''
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Alternative} & \textbf{$P$-value} \\
\midrule
$H_a:\mu>\mu_0$ & Upper tail: $P(Z\ge z_{\text{obs}})$ \\
$H_a:\mu<\mu_0$ & Lower tail: $P(Z\le z_{\text{obs}})$ \\
$H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0$ & Both tails: $2P(Z\ge |z_{\text{obs}}|)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For a symmetric null distribution, the one-sided $P$-value is exactly half the two-sided value when the effect is in the predicted direction.

\vspace{0.1cm}
Choose the direction \emph{before} seeing the data. Choosing it afterwards overstates the evidence.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Null Distribution Does Not Have to Be Normal}
The logic of a test is the same for every probability model:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State $H_0$ and $H_a$ for the parameter
  \item Use $H_0$ to fully specify the distribution of the statistic
  \item Use $H_a$ to decide which tail counts as ``at least as extreme''
  \item Add the probability of the observed outcome and every more extreme outcome
\end{enumerate}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Outcome} & \textbf{Null model} & \textbf{Practical check} \\
\midrule
Continuous measurement & Normal model for $\bar{x}$ & Design, shape, variability \\
Binary response & Binomial model for the count & Independence, common $p$ \\
Event count & Poisson model for the count & Stable rate, no clustering \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Binomial Model and Relevant Tail}
A veterinary study evaluates whether a treatment reaches a proposed response probability of $0.80$. Among $60$ independently treated dogs, $41$ respond.
$$H_0:p=0.80 \qquad H_a:p<0.80$$
Let $X$ be the number of dogs that respond. Under the null hypothesis
$$X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(60,\,0.80) \qquad E(X)=60(0.80)=48$$
We observed $41$, below $48$. The lower-tail alternative makes counts of $41$ \emph{or fewer} at least as extreme:
$$P\text{-value}=P(X\le41\mid p=0.80)$$
The vertical bar means the probability is calculated under the null model.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Exact Binomial Calculation}
Each individual probability comes from the binomial formula:
$$P(X=x\mid H_0)=\binom{60}{x}(0.80)^x(0.20)^{60-x}$$
The $P$-value adds these for every count from $0$ through $41$:
\begin{align*}
P(X\le41\mid H_0)
&=\sum_{x=0}^{41}\binom{60}{x}(0.80)^x(0.20)^{60-x} \\
&\approx 0.0221
\end{align*}
If $p=0.80$, a response count of $41$ or fewer would occur in about $2.21\%$ of comparable studies. At $\alpha=0.05$, this is evidence that $p<0.80$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Seeing the Tail}
\slidefig[0.70]{fig_ch15_binomial_tail.png}
The $P$-value is the total height of the red bars: outcomes as or more extreme than $41$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example 15.3: Exact Binomial Calculation in R}
\begin{block}{Lower-tail probability}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
# Null model: X ~ Binomial(60, 0.80)
n_dogs <- 60
p_null <- 0.80
x_observed <- 41

p_value <- pbinom(q = x_observed, size = n_dogs, prob = p_null)

p_value
# [1] 0.02206845
\end{verbatim}
\end{block}

The argument \texttt{q = 41} includes every count from $0$ through $41$. The result agrees with the exact summation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Exact vs.\ Normal Approximation}
Check the conditions: $np=48\ge10$ and $n(1-p)=12\ge10$. Under $H_0$,
$$\mu_X=48 \qquad \sigma_X=\sqrt{60(0.80)(0.20)}=\sqrt{9.6}\approx3.098$$
With a continuity correction, $X\le41$ becomes $Y\le41.5$:
$$P(X\le41)\approx P\left(Z\le\frac{41.5-48}{3.098}\right)=P(Z\le-2.10)\approx0.0180$$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{$P$-value} \\
\midrule
Exact binomial & $0.0221$ \\
Normal approximation (continuity corrected) & $0.0180$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Same conclusion, different numbers: $n(1-p)=12$ is barely above $10$. Prefer the exact calculation when it is available.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.4: Poisson Model and Relevant Tail}
A wildlife-health program historically records an average of $12$ confirmed cases of a disease per month. During one month, the program records $20$ cases.
$$H_0:\lambda=12 \qquad H_a:\lambda>12$$
Let $X$ be the number of confirmed cases in one month. Under the stable-rate null model
$$X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(12) \qquad E(X)=12$$
The upper-tail alternative makes counts of $20$ \emph{or more} at least as extreme:
$$P\text{-value}=P(X\ge20\mid\lambda=12)$$
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.4: Exact Poisson Calculation}
For a Poisson random variable,
$$P(X=x\mid H_0)=e^{-12}\frac{12^x}{x!}$$
The upper tail has infinitely many terms, so use the complement rule:
$$
P(X\ge20\mid H_0)=1-P(X\le19\mid H_0) =1-\sum_{x=0}^{19}e^{-12}\frac{12^x}{x!} \approx 0.0213
$$
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lc}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{$P$-value} \\
\midrule
Exact binomial & $0.0213$ \\
Normal approximation (continuity corrected $P(Y\geq 20.5)$) & $0.00714$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
If the mean remained $12$ cases per month, a count of $20$ or more would occur in about $2.13\%$ of months.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.4: Seeing the Tail}
\slidefig[0.70]{fig_ch15_poisson_tail.png}
Same logic as the binomial example; only the probability model has changed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example 15.4: Exact Poisson Calculation in R}
\begin{block}{Upper-tail probability}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
# Null model: X ~ Poisson(12)
lambda_null <- 12
x_observed <- 20

p_value <- ppois(q = x_observed - 1, lambda = lambda_null,
                 lower.tail = FALSE)

p_value
# [1] 0.02127977
\end{verbatim}
\end{block}

With \texttt{lower.tail = FALSE}, R calculates $P(X>19)$, which is the same event as $X\ge20$ for an integer-valued count.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.4: What the Small $P$-Value Does Not Identify}
Before concluding that the disease rate increased, check whether the Poisson model still fits:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Seasonal changes in disease occurrence
  \item Increased testing or surveillance effort
  \item Clustering within a location or animal group
  \item A change in the size of the population at risk
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{General lesson}
A small $P$-value shows tension between the data and the \emph{whole} null model. It can arise because the parameter value is wrong, because an assumption is wrong, or both.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Interpreting Statistical Significance}

\begin{frame}{Evidence Forms a Continuum}
Examples 15.3 and 15.4 gave small $P$-values. What do such results tell us?

\vspace{0.2cm}
Smaller $P$-values indicate greater incompatibility between the data and the null model. A significance level such as $\alpha=0.05$ provides a decision rule, but not a sharp scientific boundary.

\begin{block}{Example}
$P=0.048$ and $P=0.052$ represent nearly the same strength of evidence, although a fixed $5\%$ rule gives different formal decisions.
\end{block}

Report the exact $P$-value, the effect estimate and a confidence interval. The required strength of evidence depends on the plausibility of $H_0$ and the consequences of the decision.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Statistical Significance Depends on Sample Size}
For a one-sample $z$ test
$$z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}=\frac{\text{observed effect}}{\text{chance variation}}$$
The test statistic depends on the observed effect, the population variability and the sample size. Because $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ shrinks as $n$ grows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A very large sample can make a tiny effect statistically significant
  \item A small or highly variable sample can fail to detect an effect that matters
\end{itemize}
Failing to reject $H_0$ does not show the effect is zero; the study may have been too small. (Section 6 makes this precise.)
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Effect at Different Sample Sizes}
Every study observes $\bar{x}-\mu_0=0.30$ with $\sigma=2.4$.
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.66\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig_ch15_significance_sample_size.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.38\textwidth}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
$n$ & $z$ & $P$ \\
\midrule
$25$ & $0.625$ & $0.532$ \\
$100$ & $1.25$ & $0.211$ \\
$400$ & $2.50$ & $0.012$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.25cm}
The estimated effect never changes. Only the precision does.

\vspace{0.15cm}
The practical size of the effect is the same in all three studies.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Statistical Significance and Practical Importance}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.34\textwidth} X}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Useful information} \\
\midrule
Are the data difficult to explain under $H_0$? & $P$-value and model conditions \\
How large might the effect be? & Effect estimate and confidence interval \\
Would the effect matter in practice? & Scientific thresholds, consequences, costs and benefits \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
A complete analysis reports the size and uncertainty of the effect rather than only whether a significance threshold was crossed. The next example shows why.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.5: Facebook Experiment}
A randomized experiment studied whether \textbf{emotional contagion} occurs on Facebook without in person interaction. The company reduced for one week the amount of emotional (positive, negative, or neutral) content from friends in the News Feed of a random sample of $689003$ Facebook users without informing them, then monitored the users’ posts the following week.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Omitting emotional content reduced the number of words users subsequently produced:
\begin{itemize}
  \item when positivity reduced: $z=-4.78,\ P<0.001$
  \item when negativity reduced: $z=-7.219,\ P<0.001$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.1cm}
\textbf{Effect size:} on average, about \textbf{one fewer emotional word per $1000$ words} over the following week.

\textbf{Interpretation}: The results are highly statistically significant, but the effect is extremely small. The huge sample size made the test sensitive enough to detect it. The small $P$-values mean that even a change as tiny as one word in $1000$ would be unlikely in a random sample of $689{,}003$ users if changing the News Feed had \textbf{no effect}. They do not show that the effect is large or practically important.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.5: Practical Importance}
The experiment raises questions that a $P$-value cannot answer.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Does using fewer emotional words represent a meaningful change in emotion?
  \item Could users repeat language that recently appeared in their feeds?
  \item Does the experimental change resemble ordinary use of the platform?
  \item Could a small individual effect matter when applied across a large population?
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Evaluating importance requires the effect estimate, its uncertainty, the quality of the measurement and knowledge of the setting.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.6: Twenty Foods and Migraine}
A nutrition researcher surveys adults about diet and migraine frequency. For each of $20$ foods (coffee, chocolate, aged cheese, red wine, \ldots) the researcher tests
$$H_0:\text{the food is not associated with migraine}\qquad\text{at }\alpha=0.05$$

\begin{alertblock}{Suppose the truth is: \textbf{none} of the 20 foods affects migraine}
All $20$ null hypotheses are true. Every rejection is a \textbf{false positive}.
\end{alertblock}

\vspace{0.1cm}
Each test, on its own, has only a $5\%$ chance of a false positive. Assume the $20$ tests are independent.

\vspace{0.2cm}
\textbf{Question:} How likely is the researcher to ``discover'' at least one migraine food anyway?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.6: Counting False Positives Is a Binomial Problem}
Each test is a trial: false positive (probability $0.05$) or not (probability $0.95$). Let $Y$ = number of foods declared significant. Then
$$Y\sim\operatorname{Bin}(20,\ 0.05)$$
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Expected false positives:} $E(Y)=20(0.05)=1$
  \item \textbf{No false positives} (multiplication rule): $P(Y=0)=(0.95)^{20}\approx0.358$
  \item \textbf{At least one} (complement rule): $P(Y\ge1)=1-(0.95)^{20}\approx0.642$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.1cm}
About a $64\%$ chance of at least one ``significant'' food, even though no food matters.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.6: The Distribution of False Positives}
\slidefig[0.70]{fig_ch15_false_positive_count.png}
If only the one ``significant'' food is published (``Aged cheese linked to migraine, $P=0.03$''), readers never see the $19$ other tests.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Risk Grows with the Number of Tests}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.72\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig_ch15_multiple_testing.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.38\textwidth}
For $m$ independent tests of true null hypotheses:
$$1-(1-\alpha)^m$$

\vspace{0.1cm}
Each test still has Type I error probability $0.05$.

\vspace{0.1cm}
The \emph{family} chance of at least one false positive climbs quickly. Dependence changes the exact number, not the warning.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.6: Protecting Against False Discoveries}
\textbf{Bonferroni adjustment:} test each of the $m$ hypotheses at $\alpha/m$.
$$\frac{0.05}{20}=0.0025 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad 1-(1-0.0025)^{20}\approx0.049$$
The family-wide false-positive chance is back below $0.05$, but each test now needs much stronger evidence (lower power).

\vspace{0.2cm}
Practices that \emph{inflate} false positives:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{$P$-hacking / data dredging:} trying many outcomes, subgroups or models
  \item \textbf{Selective reporting:} omitting nonsignificant results
  \item \textbf{Publication bias:} significant studies are more likely to be published
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inference Within the Scientific Process}
Statistical inference supports a transparent scientific process.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the research question and analysis plan before examining the results
  \item Use a design that connects the data to the intended population or causal question
  \item Examine the observations and assess the model conditions
  \item Report all planned analyses, with estimates, uncertainty and exact $P$-values
  \item Replicate important findings with new data
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.2cm}
If ``aged cheese'' were a false positive, a new study testing only aged cheese would have just a $5\%$ chance of another significant result. Replication filters out chance findings.
\end{frame}

\section{Errors and Power}

\begin{frame}{Two Ways a Test Can Be Wrong}
The false positives in Example 15.6 are one kind of error. A test can also \emph{miss} a real effect.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
 & \textbf{$H_0$ is true} & \textbf{A specified alternative is true} \\
\midrule
Fail to reject $H_0$ & Correct decision & \textbf{Type II error} (false negative) \\
Reject $H_0$ & \textbf{Type I error} (false positive) & Correct decision \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
$$\alpha=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid H_0\text{ true}) \qquad \beta=P(\text{fail to reject }H_0\mid\text{specified alternative true})$$
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ come from \emph{different} sampling distributions. The seriousness of each error depends on context.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7: The Sweetness Study}
Storage may reduce the sweetness of a new cola. Ten trained tasters rate the cola before and after storage; the response is sweetness loss.
$$H_0:\mu=0 \qquad H_a:\mu>0 \qquad \sigma=1,\quad n=10,\quad \alpha=0.05$$
Each taster's sweetness loss is (rating before storage) $-$ (rating after storage). A mean loss of $\mu=0.8$ point means that, on average, tasters would rate the stored cola $0.8$ point \textbf{less sweet} than the fresh cola. It is the specific alternative we want the test to detect.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Step 1: write the test as a decision rule on $\bar{x}$.} Under $H_0$, $\bar{X}\sim N(0,\ 1/\sqrt{10})$, with $1/\sqrt{10}\approx0.3162$.
$$\bar{x}_{\text{crit}}=\mu_0+z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}=0+1.645(0.3162)\approx0.520$$
\begin{block}{Decision rule}
Reject $H_0$ when $\bar{x}\ge0.520$. Fail to reject $H_0$ when $\bar{x}<0.520$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7: Probability of a Type I Error}
\slidefig[0.62]{fig_ch15_type1_error.png}
If storage causes no loss ($\mu=0$):
$\quad P(\bar{X}\ge0.520\mid\mu=0)=P(Z\ge1.645)=0.05=\alpha$.
The cutoff was chosen to make this area exactly $\alpha$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7: A Type II Error in Action}
Now suppose storage \textbf{really does} cause a mean loss of $\mu=0.8$.

\vspace{0.2cm}
The ten tasters report losses with mean
$$\bar{x}=0.45$$

\begin{itemize}
  \item Decision: $0.45<0.520$, so the engineer \textbf{fails to reject} $H_0$
  \item Conclusion reported: ``not enough evidence that storage reduces sweetness''
  \item Reality: a real and important loss of $0.8$ point exists
\end{itemize}

\begin{alertblock}{This is a Type II error}
The test missed a real effect. Nothing was miscalculated: the tasters simply produced a sample mean on the low side of what $\mu=0.8$ typically gives.
\end{alertblock}

\textbf{Question:} How often would a study like this miss a loss of $0.8$?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7: Probability of a Type II Error}
\slidefig[0.60]{fig_ch15_type2_error.png}
\vspace{-0.2cm}
$$\beta=P(\bar{X}<0.520\mid\mu=0.8)=P\left(Z<\frac{0.520-0.8}{0.3162}\right)=P(Z<-0.885)\approx0.188$$
About $18.8\%$ of 10-taster studies would miss a real mean loss of $0.8$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Power: The Probability of Detecting the Effect}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.74\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig_ch15_power_curves.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
\small
$$\text{Power}=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid\text{alternative})$$
Rejecting and not rejecting are complements, so
$$\text{Power}=1-\beta$$
At $\mu=0.8$:
$$P(Z\ge-0.885)\approx0.812$$
The same cutoff gives $\alpha$ under the null curve and $\beta$ and power under the alternative curve.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Power Depends on the Alternative}
\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.72\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig_ch15_power_function.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ccc}

True $\mu$ & Power & $\beta$ \\
\midrule
$0.3$ & $0.243$ & $0.757$ \\

Larger true effects are easier to detect.

\vspace{0.1cm}
The target was power $0.90$ at $\mu=0.8$. With $n=10$ the study falls short.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.8: Type II Error with a Binomial Model}
Return to the dogs of Example 15.3: $H_0:p=0.80$ vs.\ $H_a:p<0.80$ with $n=60$.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Decision rule.} The largest $k$ with $P(X\le k\mid p=0.80)\le0.05$ is $k=42$:
$$P(X\le42\mid0.80)\approx0.0427 \qquad P(X\le43\mid0.80)\approx0.0772$$
Reject $H_0$ when $X\le42$. Because $X$ is discrete, the actual $\alpha$ is $0.0427$.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{A Type II error.} Suppose the true response probability is only $p=0.70$. A second clinic treats $60$ dogs and sees $X=45$. Since $45>42$, it fails to reject ($P$-value $\approx0.207$). The shortfall is real but missed.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{How often?}
$$\beta=P(X\ge43\mid p=0.70)=1-P(X\le42\mid p=0.70)\approx1-0.549=0.451$$
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.8: Two Binomial Distributions, One Cutoff}
\slidefig[0.74]{fig_ch15_binomial_type2.png}
\vspace{-0.15cm}
\small With 60 dogs, a drop from $0.80$ to $0.70$ would be missed almost half the time (power $\approx0.549$).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Affects Power}
The pictures show why power increases when
\begin{itemize}
  \item The sample size increases (both curves get narrower and overlap less)
  \item The true effect is farther from the null value
  \item The population variability $\sigma$ is smaller
  \item The significance level $\alpha$ is larger (cutoff moves toward $\mu_0$, more Type I errors)
  \item A one-sided test is justified instead of a two-sided test
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Trade-off}
For a fixed $n$, lowering $\alpha$ reduces Type I errors but increases Type II errors. Increasing $n$ is the main way to reduce both.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\section{Planning Studies}

\begin{frame}{Planning a Confidence Interval}
Sample size controls both precision and power. Planning uses these relationships in reverse.

\vspace{0.15cm}
Start from the margin of error and solve for $n$:
$$m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sqrt{n}=\frac{z^*\sigma}{m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n=\left(\frac{z^*\sigma}{m}\right)^2$$
\begin{itemize}
  \item $z^*$: chosen confidence level
  \item $\sigma$: from previous research, a pilot study or a conservative guess
  \item $m$: the largest acceptable margin of error
\end{itemize}
Always round \textbf{up}, so the achieved margin does not exceed the target.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.9: Planning a Body-Temperature Study}
Researchers want to estimate the mean body temperature of healthy adults within $0.05^\circ$F at $95\%$ confidence. Previous research suggests
$$\sigma=0.6^\circ\mathrm{F}\qquad\text{and}\qquad z^*=1.960$$
The required sample size is
$$n=\left(\frac{1.960(0.6)}{0.05}\right)^2=(23.52)^2=553.19$$
The study must recruit at least
$$\boxed{n=554}$$
Rounding down to $553$ would produce a margin of error slightly larger than $0.05^\circ$F.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Population Size and Required Sample Size}
The sample-size formula depends on
\begin{itemize}
  \item The confidence level through $z^*$
  \item The expected population variability through $\sigma$
  \item The desired precision through $m$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Population size does not appear when the population is much larger than the sample. A sample of $554$ gives about the same margin for a population of $100{,}000$ or $10{,}000{,}000$, if variability and design are comparable.

\vspace{0.2cm}
A finite-population correction may be used when the sample is a substantial fraction of the population.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Planning a Hypothesis Test}
Planning a test requires three decisions, made \emph{before} data collection:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Significance level $\alpha$:} the tolerated Type I error probability
  \item \textbf{Meaningful effect $\delta$:} the smallest departure from $H_0$ that matters in context
  \item \textbf{Power $1-\beta$:} the desired probability of detecting that effect
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.25cm}
These choices connect the statistical design to the consequences of declaring an effect that is not there (Type I) or missing one that is (Type II).

\vspace{0.15cm}
The effect size should come from scientific reasoning, not from the data of the planned study.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7 (continued): How Many Tasters?}
With $n=10$, power at $\mu=0.8$ was only $0.812$. For power $0.90$, the cutoff must sit $z_{\text{power}}$ SDs below the alternative mean:
$$\mu_0+z_{1-\alpha}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}=\mu_0+\delta-z_{\text{power}}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
n=\left(\frac{(z_{1-\alpha}+z_{\text{power}})\,\sigma}{\delta}\right)^2$$
With $\alpha=0.05$, power $=0.90$, $\sigma=1$, $\delta=0.8$:
$$n=\left(\frac{(1.645+1.282)(1)}{0.8}\right)^2\approx13.38 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{n=14}$$
Check: with $n=14$, power $=P\big(Z\ge1.645-0.8\sqrt{14}\big)=P(Z\ge-1.35)\approx0.911$.
\end{frame}

\begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
\begin{column}{0.60\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth,height=0.72\textheight,keepaspectratio]{fig_ch15_power_sample_size.png}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.38\textwidth}
Because $n\propto1/\delta^2$, halving the effect to be detected multiplies the required sample size by about four.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Smaller effects are harder to distinguish from sampling variation, so they need more observations.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\section{Summary}

\begin{frame}{A Consistent Inferential Workflow}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the population, parameter and scientific question
  \item Describe how the observations were selected or treatments were assigned
  \item Match the response to a probability model and check its assumptions
  \item Choose the statistic and determine its sampling distribution
  \item Calculate an interval or a test statistic and $P$-value
  \item Report the effect size, uncertainty, limitations and practical meaning
  \item Consider the error probabilities and power of the design
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Probability supports the calculation. Study design and subject-matter reasoning determine the scope of the conclusion.
\end{frame}
