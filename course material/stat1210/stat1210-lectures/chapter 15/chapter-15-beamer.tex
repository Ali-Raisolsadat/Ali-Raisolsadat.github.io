% SLIDE 2
\begin{frame}{Probability and Inference Belong Together}
Chapters 9--12 developed probability models for uncertain outcomes. Chapters 13--14 used those models to describe sampling variation and draw conclusions about populations.

\vspace{0.25cm}
Chapter 15 brings these ideas together. The same probability rules and distributions now help us decide whether an inferential conclusion is reliable and useful.

\begin{block}{Central Connection}
Probability describes what could happen under a model. Inference compares the observed data with those model-based possibilities.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 3
\begin{frame}{Chapter Roadmap}
The chapter follows the path from a probability model to a practical conclusion.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Connect probability rules and distributions to inferential reasoning
  \item Assess the study design and the assumptions behind the model
  \item Examine how confidence level, variability and sample size affect estimation
  \item Interpret hypothesis tests, error probabilities and practical importance
  \item Plan studies using a target margin of error or power
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.2cm}
The examples include continuous measurements, binary outcomes and event counts.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parameters, Statistics and Randomness}
A population parameter is a fixed but usually unknown number. A sample statistic is calculated from the observations and changes from sample to sample.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Statistic} \\
\midrule
Population mean & $\mu$ & $\bar{x}$ \\
Population probability & $p$ & $\hat p$ \\
Population event rate & $\lambda$ & Observed rate or count \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Inference uses the observed statistic to learn about the parameter. Probability describes how much the statistic could vary before the sample is selected.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sampling Distributions Supply Inferential Probabilities}
A probability model for individual outcomes does not directly describe the behaviour of a sample statistic.

\vspace{0.2cm}
The sampling distribution connects the two levels:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A population model describes possible individual outcomes
  \item Repeated random samples produce different statistics
  \item The sampling distribution describes those possible statistic values
  \item Confidence intervals and tests use that distribution to measure uncertainty
\end{enumerate}

\begin{block}{Key Distinction}
The distribution of individual observations and the sampling distribution of a statistic answer different questions.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 4
\begin{frame}{The Probability-to-Inference Process}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.20\textwidth} X >{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.25\textwidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Stage} & \textbf{Question} & \textbf{Statistical object} \\
\midrule
Probability model & What outcomes could occur if the model were correct? & Normal, binomial or Poisson distribution \\
Sampling distribution & How would a statistic vary across repeated samples? & Distribution of $\bar{x}$, $\hat p$ or a count \\
Inference & Is the observed statistic plausible under a stated population claim? & Confidence interval or $P$-value \\
Practical conclusion & What does the evidence support in the study setting? & Contextual conclusion with limitations \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Inference remains conditional on the probability model and the data-collection process.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 5
\begin{frame}{Probability Rules Inside Inference}
Several rules from Chapters 9--10 appear directly in inferential calculations.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Complement rule:} upper-tail probabilities use $P(Z\ge z)=1-P(Z<z)$
  \item \textbf{Addition rule:} a two-sided $P$-value combines probabilities from two disjoint tails
  \item \textbf{Conditional probability:} a $P$-value is calculated conditional on $H_0$ and the model assumptions being true
  \item \textbf{Independence:} standard formulas usually assume that one observation does not determine another
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Important Distinction}
$P(\text{data as or more extreme}\mid H_0)$ is not the same as $P(H_0\mid\text{data})$.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 6
\begin{frame}{The Normal Distribution in Inference}
Chapter 11 used $z$-scores to convert observations to areas under the Standard Normal curve. In inference, the same process applies to a statistic.

\[
z=\frac{\text{statistic}-\text{model value}}{\text{standard error}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item Critical values identify central areas used for confidence intervals
  \item Tail areas measure how unusual a test statistic is under $H_0$
  \item The standard error replaces the standard deviation of individual observations
\end{itemize}

The Normal model therefore connects standardization, confidence intervals and $P$-values.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 7
\begin{frame}{Binomial and Poisson Models in Inference}
The distribution should match the type of outcome being studied.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.23\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Data structure} & \textbf{Inferential question} \\
\midrule
Binomial & Number of successes in $n$ independent trials with the same success probability & Is the population success probability consistent with a proposed value? \\
Poisson & Number of events in a fixed amount of time, area or exposure under a stable rate & Is the event rate consistent with a proposed value? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The exact distribution can provide a tail probability. A Normal approximation may be used when its conditions are satisfied.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exact Models and Normal Approximations}
An inferential probability can come from the original probability model or from an approximation to a sampling distribution.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.25\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Approach} & \textbf{Advantage} & \textbf{Important condition} \\
\midrule
Exact binomial or Poisson calculation & Retains the discrete probability model & The model assumptions must fit the data-generating process \\
Normal approximation & Uses standardization and Normal tail areas & The sample or expected counts must be sufficiently large \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
The word exact refers to the calculation under the model. It does not guarantee that the model itself is correct.
\end{frame}

\section{Conditions for Inference}

% SLIDE 8
\begin{frame}{Conditions for One-Sample $z$ Procedures}
The one-sample $z$ procedures for a population mean assume that

\begin{itemize}
  \item The observations come from a random sample or a defensible random process
  \item The observations are independent
  \item The population is Normal, or the sampling distribution of $\bar{x}$ is approximately Normal
  \item The population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known
\end{itemize}

\[
\bar{x}\pm z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}
\]

Later chapters replace the uncommon known-$\sigma$ assumption with an estimate from the sample.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 9
\begin{frame}{The Source of the Data}
Probability can describe sampling variation only when the data arise from a process that the probability model represents reasonably well.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Chance can enter a study through

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Random sampling}, which supports generalization from the sample to a defined population
  \item \textbf{Random assignment}, which helps balance lurking variables and supports causal comparisons
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{First Question}
Before calculating an interval or a $P$-value, identify how the observations entered the study and which conclusion that design can support.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 10
\begin{frame}{Random Sampling and Random Assignment}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.22\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
\textbf{Design feature} & \textbf{Main purpose} & \textbf{Conclusion supported} \\
\midrule
Random sampling & Select a representative sample from a defined population & Generalization to the sampled population \\
Random assignment & Assign experimental units to treatments through chance & Stronger evidence about treatment effects \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.25cm}
A study may use one, both or neither form of randomization. The scope of the conclusion should match the design that was used.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Independence of Observations}
Many standard errors and probability models assume that the observations are independent.

\begin{itemize}
  \item In a random sample, one selected individual should not determine another individual's response
  \item Repeated measurements from the same individual are usually dependent
  \item Animals in the same litter, patients in the same clinic or measurements from nearby sites may be correlated
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
When sampling without replacement from a finite population, the condition

\[
n\leq0.10N
\]

often supports treating observations as approximately independent. Dependence may require a different analysis.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 11
\begin{frame}{Practical Problems After Data Collection Begins}
Even a well-planned study can encounter difficulties.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Nonresponse can make respondents systematically different from nonrespondents
  \item Attrition can create bias when participants who leave differ from those who remain
  \item Measurement problems can introduce systematic error outside the sampling model
  \item Clustering or unequal selection probabilities can invalidate a simple random-sample formula
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
A probability calculation cannot correct a fundamental mismatch between the study design and the statistical model.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 12
\begin{frame}{Choosing a Population Model}
The response variable determines the starting probability model.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.95\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.22\textwidth} >{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.25\textwidth} X}
\toprule
\textbf{Response} & \textbf{Possible model} & \textbf{Parameter} \\
\midrule
Continuous measurement & Normal model or a model justified through a sampling distribution & Population mean $\mu$ \\
Binary outcome & Binomial model & Population probability $p$ \\
Event count over exposure & Poisson model & Population rate $\lambda$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Model assumptions concern more than the shape of a graph. They also describe independence, a stable probability or rate and the relevant population.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 13
\begin{frame}{Sample Size and the Normal Approximation}
The amount of non-Normality that a procedure can tolerate depends partly on the sample size.

\begin{itemize}
  \item With a small sample, strong skewness or outliers can make Normal-based inference unreliable
  \item With a moderate sample, a roughly symmetric population may be sufficient
  \item With a large sample, the Central Limit Theorem supports an approximately Normal sampling distribution under broad conditions
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
There is no universal sample-size cutoff. Stronger skewness and heavier tails generally require more observations.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 14
\begin{frame}{Outliers and Nonresistant Statistics}
The sample mean $\bar{x}$ and sample standard deviation $s$ are sensitive to extreme observations.

\vspace{0.2cm}
An outlier can change the estimate, increase the standard error and alter the conclusion of a test.

\vspace{0.2cm}
Before performing inference:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Check for data-entry and measurement errors
  \item Examine a suitable graph of the observations
  \item Investigate scientifically plausible reasons for unusual values
  \item Consider another method when a small sample contains severe skewness or influential outliers
\end{itemize}

An observation should not be removed solely because its removal changes the conclusion.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 15
\begin{frame}{A Conditions Checklist}
Before reporting an inferential result, consider the following questions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item What population or treatment comparison does the study address?
  \item How were individuals selected and, when applicable, assigned to treatments?
  \item Are the observations sufficiently independent?
  \item Does the probability model match the response and the study process?
  \item Are outliers, missing observations or measurement problems present?
  \item Does the analysis account for the actual sampling or experimental design?
\end{enumerate}

These questions determine whether the standard error, confidence interval and $P$-value have a defensible interpretation.
\end{frame}

\section{How Confidence Intervals Behave}

% SLIDE 16
\begin{frame}{The General Form of a Confidence Interval}
Many confidence intervals have the form

\[
\text{estimate}\pm\text{margin of error}
\]

The margin of error combines a critical value and a standard error.

\[
\text{margin of error}=\text{critical value}\times\text{standard error}
\]

For a one-sample $z$ confidence interval for a population mean,

\[
m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

The estimate determines the centre. The margin of error determines the half-width.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From a Sampling Distribution to an Interval}
Suppose the standardized statistic follows a Standard Normal distribution:

\[
Z=\frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}
\]

For a $95\%$ procedure,

\[
P\left(-1.96\leq\frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\leq1.96\right)=0.95
\]

Rearranging the inequalities gives

\[
P\left(\bar{X}-1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\leq\mu\leq
\bar{X}+1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=0.95
\]

This probability statement describes the interval-producing procedure before the sample is observed.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 17
\begin{frame}{Normal Critical Values}
The critical value $z^*$ leaves the chosen confidence level in the centre of the Standard Normal distribution.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Confidence} & \textbf{Central area} & \textbf{Area in each tail} & \textbf{$z^*$} \\
\midrule
$90\%$ & $0.90$ & $0.05$ & $1.645$ \\
$95\%$ & $0.95$ & $0.025$ & $1.960$ \\
$99\%$ & $0.99$ & $0.005$ & $2.576$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
This is the same area reasoning used for Normal probabilities in Chapter 11. Higher confidence moves the cutoffs farther into the tails.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Long-Run Coverage}
The population parameter remains fixed. Different random samples produce different estimates and therefore different intervals.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A $95\%$ confidence procedure captures the true parameter in approximately $95\%$ of repeated random samples under the model
  \item Approximately $5\%$ of the intervals miss the parameter
  \item After one interval has been calculated, the endpoints are fixed
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Interpretation}
Confidence describes the long-run performance of the method. It does not assign a probability to the fixed parameter after the data have been observed.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 18
\begin{frame}{Confidence and Precision}
Two desirable features of an interval compete with one another.

\begin{itemize}
  \item A high confidence level gives a procedure with a high long-run coverage rate
  \item A small margin of error gives a more precise estimate of the parameter
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.25cm}
With the same data, higher confidence requires a larger $z^*$ and a wider interval.

\begin{block}{Trade-Off}
Reducing the confidence level can narrow the interval, but the procedure will capture the true parameter less often over repeated samples.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 19
\begin{frame}{Population Variability and Precision}
The margin of error increases with the population standard deviation.

\[
m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item A smaller $\sigma$ produces less sample-to-sample variation in $\bar{x}$
  \item A larger $\sigma$ makes the population mean more difficult to estimate precisely
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Natural biological variation usually cannot be removed. Measurement variation can sometimes be reduced through better instruments, procedures or training.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 20
\begin{frame}{Sample Size and the Square-Root Relationship}
For a fixed confidence level and population standard deviation,

\[
m\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Change in sample size} & \textbf{New standard error} & \textbf{Effect on margin} \\
\midrule
$n\rightarrow4n$ & One-half as large & Margin is halved \\
$n\rightarrow9n$ & One-third as large & Margin is divided by $3$ \\
$n\rightarrow100n$ & One-tenth as large & Margin is divided by $10$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Doubling the sample size multiplies the margin by $1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.707$ rather than cutting it in half.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 22
\begin{frame}{What the Margin of Error Measures}
The margin of error describes uncertainty from random sampling under the assumed model.

\vspace{0.2cm}
It reflects how far the sample statistic may fall from the population parameter because another random sample would give a different statistic.

\begin{block}{Covered by the Margin of Error}
Random sample-to-sample variation generated by the stated sampling process
\end{block}

\vspace{0.15cm}
A correctly calculated margin of error can accompany a misleading conclusion when the observations are biased or the model does not represent the study.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 23
\begin{frame}{Errors Outside the Margin of Error}
The usual margin of error does not measure uncertainty caused by

\begin{itemize}
  \item Undercoverage, voluntary response or nonresponse
  \item Systematic attrition during a study
  \item Leading questions or inaccurate self-reports
  \item Poorly calibrated instruments or inconsistent measurements
  \item Confounding in an observational study
  \item A model that does not match the design or population
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
These sources of error can exceed the reported sampling error. A narrow interval does not compensate for biased data.
\end{frame}

\section{How Hypothesis Tests Behave}

% SLIDE 24
\begin{frame}{A $P$-Value as a Model-Based Probability}
A hypothesis test temporarily assumes that the null hypothesis and model conditions are true.

\[
P\text{-value}=P(\text{statistic at least as extreme as observed}\mid H_0)
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item A small value means that the observed result lies in an unusual part of the null distribution
  \item A large value means that the observed result remains reasonably compatible with the null model
\end{itemize}

The calculation evaluates the data under $H_0$. It does not calculate the probability that $H_0$ is true.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Test Statistic Locates the Data}
For a one-sample $z$ test of a population mean,

\[
z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item The numerator is the observed difference from the null value
  \item The denominator is the standard error expected under repeated sampling
  \item The resulting $z$ value measures the difference in standard-error units
\end{itemize}

The $P$-value is the relevant tail area beyond the observed test statistic under the null distribution.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Evidence Forms a Continuum}
Smaller $P$-values indicate greater incompatibility between the observed statistic and the null model.

\vspace{0.2cm}
A significance level such as $\alpha=0.05$ provides a decision rule, but it does not create a sharp scientific distinction between nearby results.

\begin{block}{Example}
$P=0.048$ and $P=0.052$ represent nearly the same strength of evidence, although a fixed $5\%$ rule gives different formal decisions.
\end{block}

The exact $P$-value, effect estimate, confidence interval and consequences of a decision should inform the conclusion.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 25
\begin{frame}{The Alternative Hypothesis Determines the Tail}
The direction of the scientific question determines which probabilities count as at least as extreme.

\begin{itemize}
  \item $H_a:\mu>\mu_0$ uses the upper tail
  \item $H_a:\mu<\mu_0$ uses the lower tail
  \item $H_a:\mu\ne\mu_0$ combines two disjoint tails
\end{itemize}

For a symmetric null distribution,

\[
P_{\text{two-sided}}=2P(Z\ge |z_{\text{obs}}|)
\]

Choosing the direction after seeing the data understates uncertainty and does not preserve the intended error rate.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 26
\begin{frame}{Type I and Type II Errors}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.24\textwidth} X X}
\toprule
 & \textbf{$H_0$ is true} & \textbf{A specified alternative is true} \\
\midrule
Fail to reject $H_0$ & Correct decision & Type II error \\
Reject $H_0$ & Type I error & Correct decision \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\[
P(\text{Type I error})=\alpha
\qquad
P(\text{Type II error})=\beta
\]

The relative seriousness of the two errors depends on their scientific, medical or policy consequences.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 27
\begin{frame}{Statistical Significance and Sample Size}
For a one-sample $z$ test,

\[
z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}
=\frac{\text{observed effect}}{\text{chance variation}}
\]

The test statistic depends on the size of the observed effect, the population variability and the sample size.

\vspace{0.2cm}
A very large sample can identify a small effect as statistically significant. A small or highly variable sample may fail to detect an effect that matters in practice.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 29
\begin{frame}{Statistical Significance and Practical Importance}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.92\textwidth}{>{\RaggedRight\arraybackslash}p{0.30\textwidth} X}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Useful information} \\
\midrule

Would the effect matter in practice? & Scientific thresholds, consequences, costs and benefits \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.2cm}
A complete analysis reports the size and uncertainty of the effect rather than only whether a significance threshold was crossed.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 30
\begin{frame}{What a $P$-Value Does Not Mean}
A $P$-value is calculated under the null hypothesis and the model assumptions.

\vspace{0.2cm}
It is not

\begin{itemize}
  \item The probability that $H_0$ is true
  \item The probability that the result occurred only because of chance
  \item The size or importance of the observed effect
  \item A substitute for checking the study design and model conditions
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
A large $P$-value indicates compatibility with the null model. It does not establish that the null hypothesis is correct.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 31
\begin{frame}{Multiple Tests and the Complement Rule}
Suppose $m$ independent tests use significance level $\alpha$ and all null hypotheses are true.

\[
P(\text{no false positives})=(1-\alpha)^m
\]

The complement rule gives

\[
P(\text{at least one false positive})=1-(1-\alpha)^m
\]

For $m=20$ and $\alpha=0.05$,

\[
1-(0.95)^{20}\approx0.642
\]

This calculation explains why selective reporting can produce apparently significant findings even when every null hypothesis is true.
\end{frame}

Each test still has Type I error probability $0.05$.

\vspace{0.2cm}
The family-level chance of at least one false positive increases as more questions are tested.

\vspace{0.2cm}
The calculation assumes independent tests.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 33
\begin{frame}{Inference Within the Scientific Process}
Statistical inference supports a transparent scientific process.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the research question and analysis plan before examining the results
  \item Use a design that connects the data to the intended population or causal question
  \item Examine the observations and assess the model conditions
  \item Report estimates, uncertainty and exact $P$-values in context
  \item Replicate important findings with new data when possible
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Replication reduces the chance that a conclusion depends on an unusual sample, an unnoticed design problem or flexible analysis choices.
\end{frame}

\section{Planning Studies}

% SLIDE 34
\begin{frame}{Planning a Confidence Interval}
For a one-sample $z$ confidence interval,

\[
m=z^*\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
\]

Solving for the required sample size gives

\[
n=\left(\frac{z^*\sigma}{m}\right)^2
\]

The value must be rounded up so that the achieved margin of error does not exceed the target.

\vspace{0.2cm}
A plausible value of $\sigma$ can come from previous research or a pilot study.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 35
\begin{frame}{Population Size and Required Sample Size}
The usual sample-size formula depends on

\begin{itemize}
  \item The confidence level through $z^*$
  \item The expected population variability through $\sigma$
  \item The desired precision through $m$
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Population size does not appear when the population is much larger than the sample. The same planned sample size can provide the same margin of error in two large populations when their sampling designs and variability are comparable.

\vspace{0.2cm}
A finite-population correction may be used when the sample forms a substantial fraction of the population.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 36
\begin{frame}{Planning a Hypothesis Test}
Planning a test requires decisions about

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Significance level $\alpha$:} the tolerated Type I error probability
  \item \textbf{Meaningful effect:} the smallest departure from $H_0$ that matters in context
  \item \textbf{Power:} the desired probability that the test detects that effect
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.25cm}
These choices should be made before data collection. They connect the statistical design to the consequences of missing an important effect or incorrectly declaring one.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 37
\begin{frame}{Power and Type II Error}
The power of a test against a specified alternative is

\[
\text{Power}=P(\text{reject }H_0\mid\text{specified alternative is true})
\]

If $\beta$ is the Type II error probability for that alternative, then

\[
\text{Power}=1-\beta
\]

Power generally increases with a larger sample, a larger effect, lower population variability or a larger significance level. A two-sided test usually needs more observations than a one-sided test with the same target power.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.1: The Scope of Generalization}
Two researchers recruit students from introductory courses at one university.

\begin{block}{Neurobiology Study}
A researcher studies responses to an optical illusion. Basic visual mechanisms may be similar across many groups, so subject-matter knowledge may support a cautiously broader interpretation.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Attitude Survey}
A researcher studies opinions about human-participant research. Age, education, program and campus culture may affect responses, so one class does not represent adults or university students generally.
\end{block}

Neither class is a random sample. The defensible scope differs because the research questions differ.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 40
\begin{frame}{Example 15.2: Binomial Model and Relevant Tail}
A veterinary study evaluates whether a treatment reaches a proposed response probability of $0.80$. Among $60$ independently treated dogs, $41$ show the response defined in the study protocol.

\[
H_0:p=0.80
\qquad
H_a:p<0.80
\]

Let $X$ be the number of dogs that respond. Under the null hypothesis,

\[
X\sim\operatorname{Bin}(60,0.80)
\]

The lower-tail alternative makes counts of $41$ or fewer at least as extreme as the observed result. Therefore, the P-value is

\[
P(X\le41\mid H_0)
=
P(X\le41\mid p=0.80)
\]

The vertical bar means that the probability is calculated under the null model.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 41
\begin{frame}{Example 15.2: Exact Binomial Calculation}
For a binomial random variable,

\[
P(X=x\mid H_0)
=
\binom{60}{x}(0.80)^x(0.20)^{60-x}
\]

The lower-tail probability adds the probabilities for all counts from $0$ through $41$:

\begin{align*}
P(X\le41\mid H_0)
&=
\sum_{x=0}^{41}
\binom{60}{x}(0.80)^x(0.20)^{60-x} \\
&\approx 0.022068 \\
&\approx 0.0221
\end{align*}

If $p=0.80$, a response count of $41$ or fewer would occur in approximately $2.21\%$ of comparable studies.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 42
\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example 15.2: Exact Binomial Calculation in R}
\begin{block}{Lower-tail probability}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
# Null model: X ~ Binomial(60, 0.80)
n_dogs <- 60
p_null <- 0.80
x_observed <- 41

p_value <- pbinom(
  q = x_observed,
  size = n_dogs,
  prob = p_null
)

p_value
# [1] 0.02206845
\end{verbatim}
\end{block}

The argument \texttt{q = 41} includes every count from $0$ through $41$. The result agrees with the exact summation.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 43
\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Poisson Model and Relevant Tail}
A wildlife-health program historically records an average of $12$ confirmed cases of a disease per month. During one month, the program records $20$ cases.

\[
H_0:\lambda=12
\qquad
H_a:\lambda>12
\]

Let $X$ be the number of confirmed cases in one month. Under the stable-rate null model,

\[
X\sim\operatorname{Pois}(12)
\]

The upper-tail alternative makes counts of $20$ or more at least as extreme as the observed count. The P-value is

\[
P(X\ge20\mid H_0)
=
P(X\ge20\mid\lambda=12)
\]
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 44
\begin{frame}{Example 15.3: Exact Poisson Calculation}
For a Poisson random variable,

\[
P(X=x\mid H_0)
=
e^{-12}\frac{12^x}{x!}
\]

It is shorter to use the complement of the cumulative probability through $19$:

\begin{align*}
P(X\ge20\mid H_0)
&=1-P(X\le19\mid H_0) \\
&=1-\sum_{x=0}^{19}e^{-12}\frac{12^x}{x!} \\
&\approx 0.021280 \\
&\approx 0.0213
\end{align*}

If the mean remained $12$ cases per month, a count of $20$ or more would occur in approximately $2.13\%$ of comparable months.
\end{frame}

% SLIDE 45
\begin{frame}[fragile]{Example 15.3: Exact Poisson Calculation in R}
\begin{block}{Upper-tail probability}
\small
\begin{verbatim}
# Null model: X ~ Poisson(12)
lambda_null <- 12
x_observed <- 20

p_value <- ppois(
  q = x_observed - 1,
  lambda = lambda_null,
  lower.tail = FALSE
)

p_value
# [1] 0.02127977
\end{verbatim}
\end{block}

With \texttt{lower.tail = FALSE}, R calculates $P(X>19)$, which is the same event as $P(X\ge20)$ for an integer-valued count.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.4: Increasing the Sample Size}
At $95\%$ confidence, the margin for $n=31$ is

\[
m_{31}=1.960\frac{15}{\sqrt{31}}\approx5.28
\]

If the sample size increases to $62$ while $\sigma$ remains $15$, then

\[
m_{62}=1.960\frac{15}{\sqrt{62}}\approx3.73
\]

\[
\frac{m_{62}}{m_{31}}=\sqrt{\frac{31}{62}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\approx0.707
\]

Doubling the sample size reduces the margin by about $29.3\%$. A new sample would usually also change the sample mean and therefore the interval centre.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.5: A Large Social-Media Experiment}
A randomized experiment altered the emotional content shown in the News Feeds of $689{,}003$ users for one week.

\vspace{0.2cm}
The reported tests produced very small $P$-values:

\[
z=-4.78
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
z=-7.219
\qquad
P<0.001
\]

The estimated change was approximately one fewer emotional word per $1000$ words written during the following week.

\begin{block}{Interpretation}
The large sample made the analysis sensitive to a very small average change. The $P$-values provide evidence against a zero-effect model but do not establish a large effect.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Example 15.5: Practical Importance}
The social-media experiment raises questions that a $P$-value cannot answer.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Does using fewer emotional words represent a meaningful change in emotion?
  \item Could users repeat language that recently appeared in their feeds?
  \item Does the experimental change resemble ordinary use of the platform?
  \item Could a small individual effect matter when applied across a large population?
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Evaluating importance requires the effect estimate, its uncertainty, the quality of the measurement and knowledge of the setting.
\end{frame}

Researchers want to estimate the mean body temperature of healthy adults within $0.05^\circ$F at $95\%$ confidence. Previous research suggests

The study must recruit at least

\begin{frame}{Example 15.7: Power in a Sweetness Study}
A quality-control study tests

\[
H_0:\mu=0
\qquad
H_a:\mu>0
\]

with $\sigma=1$, $\alpha=0.05$, $n=10$ and a meaningful mean loss of $\mu=0.8$ point.

\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
The critical sample mean is
\[
\bar{x}_{\mathrm{crit}}
=1.645\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\right)
\approx0.520
\]
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
The power at $\mu=0.8$ is
\[
z=\frac{0.520-0.8}{1/\sqrt{10}}
\approx-0.886
\]
\[
\text{Power}=P(Z\ge-0.886)\approx0.812
\]
\end{column}
\end{columns}

The initial design does not reach the target power of $0.90$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Consistent Inferential Workflow}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Identify the population, parameter and scientific question
  \item Describe how the observations were selected or treatments were assigned
  \item Match the response to a probability model and check its assumptions
  \item Choose the statistic and determine its sampling distribution
  \item Calculate an interval or a test statistic and $P$-value
  \item Report the effect size, uncertainty, limitations and practical meaning
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{0.2cm}
Probability supports the calculation. Study design and subject-matter reasoning determine the scope of the conclusion.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Chapter 15 Summary}
Probability provides the mathematical foundation for inference.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Probability rules determine tail areas, error rates and multiple-testing risks
  \item Normal, binomial and Poisson models connect different data types to inferential questions
  \item The study design and model conditions determine whether an inferential calculation is trustworthy
  \item Confidence intervals describe effect size and sampling uncertainty
  \item Statistical significance depends on the effect, variability and sample size
  \item Study planning connects precision, meaningful effects, error probabilities and power
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}
A clear conclusion reports the estimate, uncertainty, study limitations and practical meaning together.
\end{frame}
