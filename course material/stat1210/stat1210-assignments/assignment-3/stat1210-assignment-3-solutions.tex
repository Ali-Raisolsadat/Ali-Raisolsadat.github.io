\section*{Question 1 \hfill [10 marks]}

\textbf{1. Proportions and association} \partmarks{5}
$$\hat p_{\ge 60}=\frac{18}{150}=0.120 \qquad\qquad \hat p_{<60}=\frac{126}{450}=0.280$$
In the sample, $28.0\%$ of dogs with less than $60$ minutes of daily activity were obese, compared with $12.0\%$ of dogs with at least $60$ minutes. The difference is $0.280-0.120=0.160$ ($16$ percentage points), so the lower-activity group's obesity proportion was about $0.280/0.120\approx2.333$ times as large. Lower daily activity is associated with a higher proportion of obesity in this sample.

\allocation{1 mark for each proportion; 1 mark for a numerical comparison (difference or ratio); 2 marks for describing the direction of the association in context.}

\textbf{2. Study design and limitations} \partmarks{5}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Type of study:} Observational. Activity and obesity were recorded, but no activity level was imposed on any dog.
\item \textbf{Randomization:} Neither random sampling (the clinics volunteered and owners self-selected) nor random assignment (owners, not chance, determined each dog's activity) was used.
\item \textbf{Confounding variable:} Diet. Dogs fed more calories are more likely to be obese and may also be less active. Age, breed, health conditions and owner lifestyle are also acceptable.
\item \textbf{Selection bias:} Only dogs visiting five volunteer clinics in a two-week window were eligible, so dogs that rarely see a veterinarian are undercovered. Owners who agreed to take part may also differ from those who declined.
\item \textbf{Conclusion:} Without random assignment, the association may be partly or entirely due to confounders such as diet, so the study cannot show that low activity \emph{causes} obesity. Without random sampling, the participating dogs may not represent all adult dogs in Prince Edward Island, so the proportions cannot be confidently generalized to that population.
\end{itemize}

\allocation{1 mark for observational; 1 mark for stating that neither random sampling nor random assignment was used; 1 mark for a plausible confounder with an explanation; 1 mark for a source of selection bias; 1 mark for linking the design to both the causation and the generalization limits.}

\section*{Question 2 \hfill [20 marks]}

\textbf{1. Parameter, statistic, centre and spread} \partmarks{5}

\textbf{Parameter:} $p=0.42$, the population proportion of tagged juvenile salmon that survive the route. \textbf{Statistic:} $\hat p$, the proportion of the $400$ sampled salmon that survive.
$$\mu_{\hat p}=p=0.42 \qquad\qquad \sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{(0.42)(0.58)}{400}}=\sqrt{0.000609}\approx0.024678$$
Over many random samples of $400$ salmon, the sample survival proportions would average $0.42$. A typical sample proportion would differ from $0.42$ by about $0.0247$, or $2.47$ percentage points.

\allocation{1 mark for the parameter and statistic; 1 mark for the mean; 2 marks for the standard deviation; 1 mark for interpreting both in context.}

\textbf{2. Conditions and sampling distribution} \partmarks{5}
$$np=400(0.42)=168\ge10 \qquad\qquad n(1-p)=400(0.58)=232\ge10$$
Both expected counts are at least $10$, and independence is given. Therefore
$$\hat p\ \text{is approximately Normal with mean } 0.42 \text{ and standard deviation } 0.024678$$

\allocation{1 mark for each expected count; 1 mark for stating that the condition holds; 2 marks for the distribution (shape, centre and spread).}

\textbf{3. $P(\hat p\le0.38)$} \partmarks{5}
$$z=\frac{0.38-0.42}{0.024678}\approx-1.621$$
$$P(\hat p\le0.38)\approx P(Z\le-1.62)=\boxed{0.0526}$$
If the true survival probability is $0.42$, about $5.26\%$ of samples of $400$ salmon would have a survival proportion of $0.38$ or less.

\allocation{2 marks for standardizing; 2 marks for the table area; 1 mark for the interpretation.}

\textbf{4. $P(0.40\le\hat p\le0.46)$} \partmarks{5}
$$z_L=\frac{0.40-0.42}{0.024678}\approx-0.810 \qquad\qquad z_U=\frac{0.46-0.42}{0.024678}\approx1.621$$
$$P(-0.81\le Z\le1.62)=0.9474-0.2090=\boxed{0.7384}$$
Unrounded $z$ values give $0.7386$, and either answer is acceptable.

\allocation{1 mark for each standardized boundary; 2 marks for subtracting the correct cumulative areas; 1 mark for the final probability.}

\section*{Question 3 \hfill [20 marks]}

\textbf{1. Parameter and point estimate} \partmarks{5}

$\mu$ is the population mean recovery time, in minutes, for all adult dogs that underwent the routine anaesthetic procedure in the hospital network during the study period. Its point estimate is the sample mean $\bar x=47.8$ minutes.

\allocation{3 marks for defining $\mu$ with the correct population and variable; 2 marks for identifying $\bar x$ as the point estimate.}

\textbf{2. Conditions} \partmarks{5}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Random sampling:} Satisfied. The dogs are a simple random sample.
\item \textbf{Independence:} Supported by the random sample. It is \emph{assumed} that one dog's recovery time does not affect another's, for example through shared staff or the same surgery day.
\item \textbf{$10\%$ condition:} Satisfied, because $64<0.10(1000)=100$ and the population has more than $1000$ dogs.
\item \textbf{Normal sampling distribution:} The raw data are not given, so skewness and outliers cannot be checked. With $n=64$, the Central Limit Theorem makes $\bar x$ approximately Normal unless the population is extremely skewed.
\item \textbf{Assumptions that cannot be checked:} the shape of the population, independence between dogs, and that the long-term value $\sigma=12$ minutes still applies during this study period.
\end{itemize}

\allocation{1 mark each for random sampling, independence, the $10\%$ condition and the Normal condition; 1 mark for naming at least one assumption that cannot be checked.}

\textbf{3. $95\%$ confidence interval} \partmarks{5}
$$SE=\frac{12}{\sqrt{64}}=1.500 \qquad z^*=1.960 \qquad m=1.960(1.500)=2.940$$
$$47.8\pm2.940=\boxed{(44.860,\ 50.740)\text{ minutes}}$$
We are $95\%$ confident that the \emph{population mean} recovery time for adult dogs undergoing this procedure in the network is between $44.860$ and $50.740$ minutes. The interval describes the mean, not the range of individual dogs' recovery times.

\allocation{1 mark for $SE$; 1 mark for $z^*$ and $m$; 1 mark for the endpoints; 2 marks for an interpretation about the population mean.}

\textbf{4. $99\%$ confidence interval} \partmarks{5}
$$m_{99}=2.576(1.500)=3.864 \qquad 47.8\pm3.864=\boxed{(43.936,\ 51.664)\text{ minutes}}$$
\textbf{Widths:} $95\%$ is $2(2.940)=5.880$ minutes and $99\%$ is $2(3.864)=7.728$ minutes, so the $99\%$ interval is $1.848$ minutes wider. With $\sigma$ and $n$ unchanged, higher confidence requires a larger critical value ($2.576>1.960$), which gives a larger margin of error.

\allocation{2 marks for the margin of error and endpoints; 2 marks for both widths; 1 mark for the explanation.}

\section*{Question 4 \hfill [25 marks]}

\textbf{1. Parameter and hypotheses} \partmarks{5}

$\mu$ is the population mean resting respiratory rate (breaths per minute) during recovery for adult harbour seals sedated under the revised protocol.
$$H_0:\mu=18 \qquad\qquad H_a:\mu\ne18$$
The alternative is two-sided because a mean rate that is too low or too high could both be clinically important, so the team must detect a difference in either direction.

\allocation{1 mark for $\mu$; 1 mark each for $H_0$ and $H_a$; 2 marks for the reason for a two-sided alternative.}

\textbf{2. Sample mean, standard error and test statistic} \partmarks{5}
$$\bar x=\frac{503}{25}=20.120 \qquad SE=\frac{4}{\sqrt{25}}=0.800 \qquad z=\frac{20.120-18}{0.800}=\boxed{2.650}$$
\textbf{Sign:} positive, so the sample mean is above $18$ breaths per minute. \textbf{Magnitude:} the sample mean is $2.65$ standard errors above $18$, which would be unusual if $H_0$ were true.

\allocation{2 marks for $\bar x$; 1 mark for $SE$; 1 mark for $z$; 1 mark for interpreting the sign and magnitude.}

\textbf{3. P-value, decision and conclusion} \partmarks{5}
$$P(Z\ge2.65)=1-0.9960=0.0040 \qquad P\text{-value}=2(0.0040)=\boxed{0.0080}$$
The one-tail area is doubled because $H_a$ counts results as extreme in either direction. Since $0.0080<0.05$, \textbf{reject $H_0$}. At $\alpha=0.05$, there is statistically significant evidence that the population mean respiratory rate under the revised protocol differs from $18$ breaths per minute. The sample suggests that it is higher.

\allocation{2 marks for the two-sided P-value with the doubling shown; 1 mark for comparing it with $\alpha$; 1 mark for the decision; 1 mark for the conclusion in context.}

\textbf{4. Type I and Type II errors} \partmarks{5}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Type I:} The team concludes that the revised protocol changes the mean respiratory rate when the true mean is actually $18$.
\item \textbf{Type II:} The team concludes that there is no evidence of a change when the revised protocol actually shifts the mean away from $18$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{More serious:} The Type II error is more serious for animal safety. It would leave a protocol that alters breathing, possibly to dangerous levels, in use without anyone noticing. A Type I error mainly leads to unnecessary caution or the loss of a safe protocol. Accept the Type I error as more serious if the student explains a clear safety consequence.

\allocation{2 marks for each error in context; 1 mark for choosing one and justifying the choice.}

\textbf{5. Confidence interval and clinical importance} \partmarks{5}
$$m=1.960(0.800)=1.568 \qquad 20.120\pm1.568=\boxed{(18.552,\ 21.688)\text{ breaths per minute}}$$
The value $18$ is \textbf{not} in the interval. This agrees with rejecting $H_0$, because a two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$ rejects exactly when $\mu_0$ lies outside the $95\%$ interval.

\textbf{Clinical importance:} No. Statistical significance only shows that the mean is unlikely to be exactly $18$. The interval puts the increase at about $0.6$ to $3.7$ breaths per minute, and veterinarians must judge whether a change of that size affects seal health.

\allocation{1 mark for $m$; 2 marks for the endpoints; 1 mark for the agreement with the test; 1 mark for explaining that significance is not clinical importance.}

\section*{Question 5 \hfill [25 marks]}

\textbf{1. Test} \partmarks{5}

$\mu$ is the population mean difference (portable reading minus reference reading, in mg/dL) for canine blood samples.
$$SE=\frac{2.4}{\sqrt{400}}=0.120 \qquad z=\frac{0.30-0}{0.120}=\boxed{2.500} \qquad P\text{-value}=2(1-0.9938)=\boxed{0.0124}$$
Since $0.0124<0.05$, \textbf{reject $H_0$}. There is statistically significant evidence that the portable meter's mean bias is not zero.

\allocation{1 mark each for $\mu$, $SE$, $z$, the P-value and the decision.}

\textbf{2. $95\%$ confidence interval} \partmarks{5}
$$m_{95}=1.960(0.120)=0.2352 \qquad 0.30\pm0.2352=\boxed{(0.0648,\ 0.5352)\text{ mg/dL}}$$
The value $0$ is outside the interval, so the two-sided test at $\alpha=0.05$ rejects $H_0:\mu=0$, which matches Part 1.

\allocation{1 mark for $m$; 2 marks for the endpoints; 2 marks for linking the interval to the test.}

\textbf{3. Statistical significance and practical importance} \partmarks{5}

The result is \textbf{statistically significant}: $P=0.0124$ and the interval excludes $0$, so the meter very likely has some positive bias. It is \textbf{not practically important}: every value in $(0.0648,\ 0.5352)$ is below the laboratory's $1.0$ mg/dL threshold, so even the largest plausible bias is too small to matter. The large sample ($n=400$) made a small bias detectable.

\textbf{Systematic error not covered by the margin of error:} for example, the samples were \emph{stored}, and storage can change glucose levels, so the bias in fresh clinical samples may differ. Calibration drift in either instrument or inconsistent sample handling are also acceptable.

\allocation{1 mark for statistical significance; 1 mark for not practically important; 2 marks for using the interval and the $1.0$ mg/dL threshold; 1 mark for a valid source of systematic error.}

\textbf{4. $99\%$ confidence interval} \partmarks{5}
$$m_{99}=2.576(0.120)=0.30912 \qquad 0.30\pm0.30912=\boxed{(-0.00912,\ 0.60912)\text{ mg/dL}}$$
The $99\%$ interval (width $0.61824$) is wider than the $95\%$ interval (width $0.4704$) because of the larger critical value. It now contains $0$, so the test at $\alpha=0.01$ does \textbf{not} reject $H_0$, which matches $P=0.0124>0.01$. The decision changes because the P-value lies between the two significance levels: $0.01<0.0124<0.05$.

\allocation{1 mark for $m$; 2 marks for the endpoints; 1 mark for the width comparison; 1 mark for explaining why the decision differs.}

\textbf{5. Sample size for $90\%$ power} \partmarks{5}
$$n=\left(\frac{(1.645+1.282)(2.4)}{0.6}\right)^2=(2.927\times4)^2=(11.708)^2\approx137.077$$
$$\boxed{n=138\text{ blood samples}}$$
The sample size must be a whole number, and $n=137$ would give slightly less than $90\%$ power. Rounding up guarantees at least the required power.

\allocation{2 marks for the substitution; 2 marks for the arithmetic; 1 mark for rounding up with the reason.}

\section*{Bonus Question 1 \hfill [+2 marks]}

\textbf{a. Test and confidence interval} \hfill\textbf{[1 mark]}
$$SE=\frac{2.4}{\sqrt{64}}=0.300 \qquad z=\frac{14.2-15}{0.300}\approx-2.667 \qquad P\text{-value}=P(Z\le-2.67)=\boxed{0.0038}$$
Since $0.0038<0.05$, \textbf{reject $H_0$}. There is evidence that the mean cooling time is less than $15$ minutes.
$$14.2\pm1.960(0.300)=14.2\pm0.588=\boxed{(13.612,\ 14.788)\text{ minutes}}$$

\allocation{1 mark for a correct $SE$, $z$, P-value, decision and interval; deduct the mark if more than one of these is wrong.}

\textbf{b. Interpretation and limitation} \hfill\textbf{[1 mark]}

The result is \textbf{statistically significant but not practically important}. The interval suggests that the mean time is only $15-14.788=0.212$ to $15-13.612=1.388$ minutes below $15$ minutes. That is well short of the $2$-minute reduction that would matter.

\textbf{Limitation:} With one brand and one freezer, the results apply only to those conditions. Other cans, beer volumes, and freezer temperatures and loads (which the video did not report) could give very different times. \textbf{More cans would not fix this.} A larger sample under the same conditions narrows the interval by reducing random sampling error, but it still says nothing about other brands or freezers. That would require testing a variety of brands and freezers.

\allocation{1 mark for ``significant but not practically important'' with the interval used as evidence, plus the limitation and why more cans under the same conditions do not remove it.}
